In document processing and digital publishing, a "born-digital PDF" refers to a PDF file that was created directly on a computer using software (such as LaTeX, Microsoft Word, Adobe InDesign, or a digital printing engine) rather than created by physically scanning paper documents.

Why This Distinction Matters in Your Project Pipeline
Perfect Geometry & Zero Physical Artefacts: Because born-digital PDFs were never physically printed or passed through a flatbed scanner, they don't have real-world physical flaws. They have:
- No skew or tilt: The pages are perfectly aligned to the canvas (which is why your form notes that page aspect ratios match the PDF's declared MediaBox dimensions exactly).
- No scanning noise: There is no dust, paper grain, edge clipping, shadows, or "foxing" (brown staining on old paper).

Why You Rasterise Them:
In a normal digital PDF, the text is already stored as readable vector characters that you can highlight or copy.
However, your project instructions state that every team must solve a Document RAG / OCR task from page images.

## SECTION 2 — Our Corpus

**Scan & script difficulty**

Standard OCR (and even most general-purpose OCR-free VLMs) assumes a single left-to-right reading order and a font-glyph vocabulary tuned for prose. Math notation breaks both assumptions in ways specific to our corpus:

- **Superscript/subscript collapse**: exponents (`x^2`), indices (`a_n`), and limit bounds (`∫₀^π`) sit at a different baseline offset from the main text line. Line-based OCR (Tesseract, most ABBYY-style engines) merges them onto the main baseline, turning `x²` into `x2` or `x^2` into `x2` with no way to recover which reading is correct — this is a genuine ambiguity loss, not just a formatting nuisance, because `x2` is indistinguishable from a variable named `x2`.
- **Fraction bar misinterpretation**: a fraction is a *2D spatial relation* (numerator above, denominator below, divided by a horizontal rule) not a linear token sequence. Sequential OCR reads top-to-bottom-then-left-to-right within a line band, so it either drops the fraction bar entirely (producing `−b±√(b²−4ac)2a` with no division marker) or reads numerator and denominator as two unrelated lines.
- **Multi-line derivation scrambling**: a worked derivation (e.g., completing the square across 4 aligned `=` steps) relies on vertical alignment of the `=` signs to convey "these are equivalent expressions," which is a layout cue, not a text cue. Reading order algorithms tuned for paragraphs will interleave derivation lines with adjacent margin annotations or numbering, scrambling the logical step order.
- **Spatial layout vs. sequential reading order**: our corpus mixes multi-column problem sets (Siyavula) with single-column proof-heavy prose (OpenStax) on facing pages. A single global reading-order heuristic (e.g., naive top-to-bottom, left-to-right on the whole page) will interleave column A's Question 4 with column B's Question 4 continuation.
- **Diagram/inline-symbol conflation**: geometric diagrams (Siyavula trigonometry) and function plots (OpenStax) contain embedded labels (angle marks, axis ticks) that a generic OCR engine tries to read as body text, injecting garbage tokens mid-sentence.

This is why Section 1's data-speciality justification forces a **Math-Aware Vision-Language OCR stage** (Nougat-style or LaTeX-OCR) rather than a general OCR engine, and why Stage 2 (Layout) cannot be skipped for us even though our PDFs are clean born-digital renders — the problem isn't scan quality, it's notation topology.
